The assignment allows AI tools and makes the candidate responsible for every line submitted. This
report says which tools I used, what they produced, what I did and decided myself, how the AI's
work was checked, and where I stand in understanding it.

## 2. How the work was split

The project started in a "mentor" mode (`CLAUDE.md`): the AI explains and I write the code. On
27 October I switched to a "build" mode and asked Claude Code to implement the product to the
assignment's requirements. From then on **most of the source code, tests, scripts and documents
were generated by the AI**, under my direction, approvals and review.

**Why I switched.** Building a complete AI investigation engine (ingestion, hybrid search, a
multi-step RAG pipeline, an evaluation and a free deployment), analysing it and writing the full
reports in about six days was, in my judgement, not realistic for someone coming from outside AI
engineering. I am an iOS developer and I was not starting from zero, but I could not have owned
every part alone in that time. I also believe businesses now expect more work done in less time,
with AI as part of the team. So I chose to have the product and its documents built with AI first,
with me directing and checking the work, and then to study it step by step. In practice the
ownership was shared: me, plus my OpenAI and Anthropic subscriptions.

## 4. Suggestions accepted, rejected or corrected

**Accepted**: hybrid search with RRF; the registry as the single source of truth; a second
search before abstaining; fitting the server into 512 MB (ONNX embeddings, compact BM25, a
single-page UI); fallback models when the daily LLM quota runs out; fixing the evaluation
failures and re-running it. At the level of the overall design, I understood and agreed with
these. Studying them in detail is in progress (section 6).

**Rejected or changed by me**: paid upgrades (Hugging Face PRO, Oracle Pay-As-You-Go): free tiers
only. The first learning guide was one long document; I asked for a visual one with arrows,
charts and keywords. I asked for an Analytics page that the AI had not proposed.

**Problems in the AI's work that were found and fixed** (all real, from this project):
- It assumed services were still free or available: `gemini-2.5-flash` was closed to new keys,
  Hugging Face Spaces now needs PRO for Docker, and Groq's `llama-3.3-70b-versatile` was not
  available to the key. Each was found by a failed call and replaced.
- The tokenizer split `v2.18` into `v2` and `18`, so a search for v2.18 also matched v2.19. A
  test caught it; identifiers are now kept whole.
- The first searches used up all the evidence slots, so follow-up searches could not add the
  missing hop. Found while analysing evaluation failures; fixed with reserved slots.
- Two synthetic "conflicting" documents were not actually contradictory. Found in the failure
  analysis; rewritten (and disclosed in the evaluation report).
- The LLM client retried for minutes on a used-up **daily** quota, which hung an evaluation run
  and the UI. Diagnosed with a stack watchdog and a direct API call; now it fails fast or falls
  back to another model.
- One commit was pushed while a test was failing (the test itself was wrong). Fixed in the next
  commit; commits are now made only when the test suite passes.
- A test count was misreported (61 instead of 60) and corrected.
- Model prices on the Analytics page come from third-party price lists that disagree in places;
  the page says so and links each provider's own page.
- Known open limitation: dotted identifiers are kept whole, so `pool` does not match `pool.max`.

## 5. How the AI's output was verified

- 64 automated tests, including hand-computed BM25, RRF and cost examples, and tests that compare
  the compact BM25 index with the simple one.
- Measurements instead of assumptions: dataset counts, memory per component, identical vectors
  (ONNX vs PyTorch, cosine 1.00000), identical top-10 results on 500 questions.
- Reconciliation of record and chunk counts between the registry and Qdrant (local and cloud).
- Evaluation on 46 questions (two configurations, two runs) and retrieval on 500, with failure
  analysis.
- Live checks on the deployed app, including a persistence test across a container replacement.
- My own checks: uploading documents and asking questions in the live app, watching the
  deployment and the evaluation runs, and reading the reports and the learning guide.

## 7. Reflection

What helped me most was speed: a working, tested and deployed system in a few days, which I could
not have built alone in that time. The explanations (the learning guide and the walk-throughs of
RAG, BM25, RRF and embeddings) and the evaluation (metrics, question bank, failure analysis) were
the other big gains. The AI was also wrong or overconfident several times. It assumed free tiers
and models that no longer existed, and it once pushed a commit with a failing test. Checking its
claims against real calls, tests and measurements was necessary every time. Ownership of this
project is honestly shared between me and the AI tools I used; my part was direction, decisions,
setup, hosting and testing, and my next task is to understand every module well enough to change
it myself.

A personal opinion, offered as feedback and not as criticism: for candidates new to AI
engineering, building a whole new engine and writing all the reports in six days pushes them
towards having AI build it. I think a task that asks candidates to research existing models and
systems, for example to analyse the search strategy of an existing RAG system and argue how and
why they would change it, would show their understanding more directly.
